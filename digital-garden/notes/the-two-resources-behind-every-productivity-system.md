People say "I don't have enough time." But everyone gets the same 24 hours. The scarce resources aren't time — they're **energy** and **attention**. Time is just the container they exist in. An hour with full energy and undivided attention is worth more than a full day without either.

## Three invisible drains

Three things silently eat energy and attention:

1. **Decisions** — every choice, no matter how trivial, draws from a finite pool. One costs nothing. Hundreds compound into afternoon fog. This is decision fatigue.
2. **Context switches** — shifting between task types costs 10-15 minutes of residual attention per transition. You're never fully *in* anything.
3. **Open loops** — every unresolved commitment runs in the background like a browser tab you can't see, consuming RAM whether you attend to it or not.

The most dangerous costs are the ones you never notice paying.

## Two responses

Every productivity system ever built does one of two things:

### Defense — stop the bleeding

Move decisions out of the moment into a structure you set once. Three shapes:

- **Pre-commitment** — decide in advance what fills a time block. The decision is made once, not daily.
- **Batching** — group similar tasks so you pay the switching cost once instead of many times.
- **Defaults** — set a standing answer so most decisions never reach you.

### Capacity — fill the tank

Defense protects what you have. Capacity determines how much you start with. Three sources:

- **Sleep** — restores mental energy and decision quality. Set a *bedtime* alarm, not a wake-up alarm.
- **Physical activity** — costs energy now, increases total capacity over time. Clears mental loops.
- **Recovery** — fun and recreation are maintenance on the machine, not rewards for productivity.

Capacity sits *underneath* defense, not beside it. A perfect fortress around an empty tank protects nothing.

## The 8 systems are instances

Sabri Suby's 8 business systems map cleanly onto these two principles:

| System | Principle | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Money Hours | Defense | Pre-commitment for highest-impact work |
| Batch Days | Defense | One switching cost per task type |
| No Meetings Before Lunch | Defense | Protect peak-energy hours |
| Kill Worthless Decisions | Defense | Eliminate micro-decisions at design time |
| 10,000 Steps | Capacity | Regenerate energy, clear mental loops |
| Go-to-Bed Alarm | Capacity | Restore decision quality overnight |
| Book the Fun | Capacity | Prevent burnout, sustain long-term play |
| Default No | Defense | Stop other people's priorities filling your time |

Two principles, three shapes each, applied to different areas of life. No list to memorize.

## The priority trap

These systems clear the runway but never choose what flies. Without an explicit priority hierarchy, you fill protected time with **productive-but-not-important** work — tasks that feel like progress but never touch the hard, scary, truly important thing.

Productive ≠ important. The important thing gets the *first* protected block, not the leftover one. Name it. Write it down. Give it the first slot. Then build the fortress around it. [The Eisenhower Matrix](/notes/the-eisenhower-matrix/) is one framework for forcing this distinction — a 2×2 that separates urgent from important before you act.

## The design framework

1. **Name the important thing** — one thing, not ten. Before any scheduling.
2. **Audit your drains** — map last week's time to important / productive / drain.
3. **Build the system** — priority first (first slot), defense second (batch and default everything else), capacity third (lock in sleep, exercise, recovery).

Spot a drain → apply defense. Spot an energy gap → apply capacity. Spot avoidance → force the priority question.
