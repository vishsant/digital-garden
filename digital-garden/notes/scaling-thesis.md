The scaling thesis is deceptively simple: on a multi-year horizon, AI gets more intelligent as you scale it to larger computers. More compute → more capability, reliably.

OpenAI internalized this deeply around 2017. They kept seeing the same pattern across different research projects — when they scaled up compute, things got better. Not sometimes. Consistently. This wasn't supposed to happen so cleanly. The conventional wisdom was that clever algorithms mattered more than raw compute. But the data said otherwise.

This led to a crucial strategic shift. OpenAI went from being a research lab exploring many directions to one laser-focused on a small number of very scalable approaches. They sought far more compute than originally planned. The thesis reshaped everything — fundraising, partnerships, research priorities.

Here's the part that challenges the "clever algorithms" narrative: Pachocki argues that meaningful algorithmic progress tends to *correlate with* access to compute. The breakthroughs aren't independent of scaling — they're "discoveries along the path of scaling." You find new tricks *because* you're running bigger experiments. The compute comes first; the algorithmic insights follow.

This connects directly to [[Grown Not Designed]]. The scaling thesis is basically saying: grow these systems bigger and they get smarter. But "bigger" also means "harder to understand." Every step up in capability is also a step further from human ability to fully comprehend what the system is doing internally.

And it feeds directly into [[Recursive Self-Improvement]]. If compute drives progress, and AI can increasingly contribute to making compute more effective (better algorithms, better hardware design, better training methods), then the feedback loop between AI capability and compute effectiveness starts to compound. The scaling thesis isn't just about throwing money at GPUs — it's about a trajectory that, once AI enters the loop, could accelerate beyond human-paced oversight.
